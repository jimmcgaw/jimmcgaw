### What kinds of impacts have you had in your past roles?

<!-- summary -->
As a software engineering leader, I work to reduce operational costs and reduce risks.

<!-- details -->
I've often heard that you can do one of three things for a company:

1. Increase revenue
2. Reduce risk (or, to put it another way, protect revenue)
3. Reduce costs.

Since I'm not particularly skilled in sales, I contribute to the top line (#1) primarily by working with product owners to establish priorities, execute, and ship value to customers.

I contribute to #2 by esnuring organizational governance policy is applied to systems I own. In my last role, I coordinated with the compliance team to ensure we passed SOC 2 audits.

Much of my time has been focused on #3: reduce costs. I've done this by centralizing ownership of diffuse responsibilities, streamlining them, and then delegating or automating them.

How does this work? In my last role, as a tech lead, I used my initial 1:1's with teammates to learn the sources of frustration and time spent. One common thread: the release process. I took ownership of the release process so my teammates could focus on features and bug fixes, made changes so we could ship faster, and built systems to facilitate running the process, so it was either automatic or easier to onboard others to do. My 1:1's offered the success metric: over time, I stopped hearing complaints about our releases from my teammates.

### What is your philosophy about using AI to write code?

<!-- summary -->
My philosophy about using AI to assist me as an engineer is well-summarized in a old Ross Perot quote about leadership: _"Never ask anyone to do what you haven't done before and wouldn't do again"_. (Swap in "AI" for "anyone".)

<!-- details -->
Basically, <a href="https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/" target="_blank">AI is a leaky abstraction</a>. Developers build abstractions to hide complexity, and they work with them every day. The responsible use of AI for creating software systems involves your own comprehension of at least one or two abstraction layers below the level at which the AI is building.

### What is the killer use case for AI in your career?

<!-- summary -->
For me, the killer use case for AI is how it enables quickly learning and absorbing new things that I can put into practice right away.

<!-- details -->
In my experience, the best engineers are T-shaped. That is, they have broadly have some knowledge about many adjacent domains, like product management, governance, or marketing. They also have deep knowledge about a few select topics. I've spent years learning about entrepreneurship by engaging with startup communities, but now, with AI, the pace at which I can learn about security and governance frameworks, digital marketing, how to validate business ideas, and so on, is staggering. It's exciting that much of the engineering "grunt work" is handled by AI, so I can focus more time on these other things.

### What new technical direction are you most interested in these days?

<!-- summary -->
How new AI regulations and legislation can be quickly translated into auditable, reviewable policy code that immediately changes the operational characteristics of production systems.

<!-- details -->
In college, I learned the principles of auditing systems, based on frequentist statistics. This entails sampling, analyzing the sample, and merely _hoping_ that your sample is representative of the broader population. This approach always bothered me. I'm excited that in the coming years, PolicyOps will enable transactional systems to review pending transactions in real time, and allow / reject them based on responses from policy-as-code systems.
